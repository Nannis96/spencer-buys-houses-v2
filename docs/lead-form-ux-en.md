# User Experience of the Lead Capture Form

## 1. Objective

Evaluate, from a user experience (UX) and interface design (UI) perspective, the complete journey a visitor goes through when requesting a cash offer for their property: from entering their address to booking an appointment with the team.

---

## 2. Diagnosis

The form is designed to **collect information about the house**, not to **capture a contact**. It asks users for their greatest effort up front (some 20 questions about the property) and for their contact details at the very end, almost as a closing formality.

The consequence is that the costliest scenario — a genuinely interested user who tires halfway through — leaves nothing usable behind: they don't get their offer, and the business gets no way to reach them.

The fixes that follow require no change to the visual identity or the value proposition. They are adjustments of **order, clarity and transparency**, and can be applied incrementally.

---

## 3. What works well

Worth stating before the corrections: the foundation is solid, and there's no need to start from scratch.

- **Getting started is genuinely easy.** Address autocomplete lets users begin by typing just a few letters — the right first impression.
- **Solid mobile fundamentals:** each field opens the appropriate keyboard (numeric for figures, telephone for the phone number).
- **Consent is well placed**, right before submission, next to the action it authorizes.
- **The progress indicator is honest:** it reflects the user's real progress rather than being decorative.
- **The system does work on the user's behalf:** it looks up public property records on its own and fills them in (though it never says so — see finding 4).

---

## 4. What needs fixing

Each finding appears once, with its impact and its recommended action. The order is the priority.

### High priority

| Finding | Impact on the user | Recommended action |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Contact details are requested at the very end** of the flow, after every question about the property | Anyone who abandons before the last step loses all their effort: they get no offer, and the business is left with no way to reach them. The biggest commitment is demanded at the worst possible moment *(effort–reward sequencing)* | Move first name and phone to the opening screens, immediately after the address |
| **2. The improvements/repairs question mixes two concepts**: it asks what has been upgraded, but offers options that read like outstanding work | Users hesitate between "what I fixed" and "what still needs fixing," and end up answering something that doesn't reflect their situation *(clarity of language)* | Rewrite the question and its options so they communicate a single idea |
| **3. "How long have you owned the property?" is a free-text field** with no suggested format | Users don't know whether to enter a number of years, a date, or an explanation; it's one of the slowest questions to answer *(recognition over recall)* | Replace it with predefined options: less than 1 year / 1–5 years / more than 5 / inherited |

### Medium priority

| Finding | Impact on the user | Recommended action |
|---|---|---|
| **4. Auto-filled data isn't identified as such** | Users can't tell what they typed from what was already populated: they may distrust it and retype it, or let an incorrect value through without reviewing it *(system transparency)* | Flag those fields visually and invite users to **confirm** them, rather than fill them in from scratch |
| **5. A required field contradicts its own helper text**, which says "even a rough estimate is helpful" | Unjustified friction: the form demands precision for something it itself presents as flexible *(consistency between message and behavior)* | Make it optional, or offer a suggested, editable value |
| **6. The progress indicator doesn't say how much is left** and drops its text labels on small screens | On the most-used device, users move forward without knowing how much lies ahead *(reducing uncertainty)* | Keep the labels visible on mobile and add a concrete reference: "Step 3 of 6" |
| **7. One screen packs a map, an address summary and nine questions** into a single long scroll | Visual overload and fatigue; it's the point in the flow with the highest risk of abandonment through sheer tiredness *(cognitive load / progressive disclosure)* | Break it into lighter blocks and present already-known data as a compact summary |
| **8. The opening promise of "approximately 2 minutes"** doesn't match the real flow | The expectation breaks halfway through, once the user has already invested effort — the most expensive moment to lose their trust *(honest expectations)* | Align the message with the real duration, or shorten the flow until the promise is true |

### Low priority

| Finding | Impact on the user | Recommended action |
|---|---|---|
| **9. If users get interrupted, they can't resume**, and get no signal that their progress was saved | They have to start from scratch, and in practice they don't come back *(user control and task continuity)* | Let users pick up where they left off — and tell them they can |
| **10. Questions don't adapt to previous answers** | Everyone walks through the same screens even when their situation already rules certain questions out *(efficiency of use)* | Hide questions that no longer apply |

---

## 5. What we gain

- **More contacts**, including from users who never reach the end of the flow.
- **Better data quality**, by removing ambiguous questions and free-text answers.
- **Greater trust**: users understand where they are, how much is left, and which data is already filled in.
- **Earlier sales follow-up**, without depending on the user completing absolutely everything.

---

## 6. Conclusion

The highest-impact change is also the simplest to explain: **ask first for what keeps the conversation going (name and phone), and only afterwards for what refines the offer.**

Findings 2 and 3 improve data quality at low effort. The medium- and low-priority items are refinements that make the flow clearer and shorter, but none of them on its own solves the main risk: losing entirely the user who doesn't make it to the end.
