# Final Report — Cash Offer Request Form

**Spencer Buys Houses** · Pre-launch review
August 4, 2026

---

## Scope

We reviewed the complete journey a visitor takes from typing their address on the home page to booking their appointment: all seven form steps, the contact screen, and the scheduling page.

This review takes the business decisions already made as given and **does not challenge them**. Where a user-experience recommendation conflicts with a business decision, we assess how well that decision is implemented — not whether it should exist.

---

## Executive summary

The redesign achieved what it set out to do. The form went from four crowded screens holding roughly twenty fields to **seven steps with a single question each**. The journey is shorter, clearer, and considerably easier to complete on a phone.

The one issue of consequence we found is not in the design of the journey. It is the gap between **what the form promises and what the business can responsibly deliver**. It is a copywriting problem, not a structural one, and it is fixed by changing the text on two screens.

> ### Overall rating: **8.5 / 10**
>
> The structure of the form is settled. What separates the current score from a 9.5 is one expectation adjustment and two low-cost refinements.

## What works very well

**One decision per screen.** This is the highest-value change in the entire redesign. The visitor never sees more than one question at a time, and no screen forces them to scroll before they understand what is being asked.

**The system does the work for the customer.** As soon as the address is known, the form looks up the property's public records in the background. By the time the visitor reaches the details step, the information is already waiting for them — they simply confirm it instead of typing it.

**The customer always has the final word.** If the visitor entered a value, the system never overwrites it with the one it found. And the moment they edit a suggested value, the "From public records" tag disappears — the form stops taking credit for something that now belongs to the customer.

**The progress indicator is honest.** The line "Step 4 of 7 — Price" is always visible, including on phones where the bar's labels are hidden for lack of space. The visitor always knows where they are and how much is left.

**Questions that no longer apply are not asked.** The year of the last sale only appears if public records actually knew it. The customer is never asked to recall a date there is no reason to expect them to know.

**Progress is not lost.** If the visitor goes back from the contact screen to correct something, their answers are still there — in both directions.

## The item to resolve before launch

### 1. The form promises an instant offer the business should not give online

**What is happening.** The journey promises an instant offer at two points:

| Where | What it says |
|---|---|
| Home page | "Receive your offer in approximately **2 minutes**" |
| Final screen | "One More Step — **To See Your OFFER**" |

After submitting the form, the visitor lands on the scheduling page and reads: *"You will receive your offer via email shortly"*, followed by a video and a calendar for booking the conversation.

**Why it matters.** This is a broken promise at the point of the customer's greatest investment. They have just answered six questions and handed over their name, phone number, and email — and the reason they did so was *to see the offer now*.

It is also the one thing that turns a sound business decision into a risk. Placing contact details at the end is fine **as long as the exchange is honored**. When it is not, the customer has paid the form's highest price and feels they got nothing in return.

**What we propose.** Align the copy with what the business actually delivers. **We do not recommend publishing a figure on the site** — and not merely for operational reasons. Showing one would be a commercial mistake.

- A cash offer on a house **cannot be stood behind without seeing the property**. Any number published before the visit is provisional by definition.
- A provisional number **anchors the seller**. If the real offer comes in lower, the conversation opens on the defensive instead of opening on the sale.
- A figure that is later revised downward **reads as bait**. The reputational cost outweighs any gain in conversion.
- The figure is **the customer's best reason to show up to the appointment**. Handed over in advance, it stops being one.

The scheduling page already says the right thing. The work is getting the screens before it to say the same:

| Where | Change to |
|---|---|
| Home page | "Receive your offer **within 24 hours**" |
| Final screen | "One More Step — **To Receive Your Offer**" |

The final button, *"GET MY FREE CASH OFFER"*, can stay exactly as it is: it states the customer's intent, not a timeframe.

| Impact | Effort |
|---|---|
| **High** | Low |

## Improvements recommended after launch

Neither of these blocks publishing. Both are low cost.

### 2. The "Other" option asks nothing further

When a customer selects *Other* as their reason for selling, the sales team receives literally the word "Other". We propose that selecting it reveals an optional one-line text field. It is valuable information at zero cost: anyone who does not want to write simply moves on.

**Impact: Medium · Effort: Low**

### 3. On the address screen, the map appears before the address

The map fills the entire first screen on a phone, pushing the address text — the thing that actually needs verifying — below it. Reading order should match decision order: the data first, the visual confirmation after.

**Impact: Medium · Effort: Low**

---

## What we would do before calling the form finished

**One thing: adjust the copy on the two screens that promise an instant offer.** It is a wording change with no technical risk, and it removes the only point in the journey where the customer can feel misled.

The two remaining improvements can be implemented after publishing, in any order.

---

## About the rating

**8.5 out of 10.**

What already earns a 9 is the journey itself: the order of the questions, how little is asked of the customer on each screen, the automatic use of public records, the honesty of the progress indicator, and the visual consistency of the whole. As a form structure, this is settled.

What brings the score down:

| Item | Deducts | Why |
|---|---|---|
| The instant-offer promise | −1.0 | It is the only thing that directly affects customer trust |
| The two outstanding refinements | −0.5 | Polish: neither prevents anyone from completing the form |

Reaching a 10 would require features whose return is still uncertain — saving progress so it can be resumed later, deeper conditional logic — and we do not recommend pursuing them for now. Perfecting without evidence costs more than it returns.
